New York Cut Workers' Comp Rates 22%. Your Bill Might Not Move.

On October 1, 2026, New York approved a 21.9% reduction in workers' comp loss cost rates. The headlines promise big savings. But the cut is a statewide average, and your premium is set by your class codes, your payroll, and your experience mod. Here is what the cut actually changes, and the three things that can eat all of it.

This article explains the October 2026 New York rate change in general terms and is not insurance or legal advice. Rate filings, class relativities, and carrier programs change; confirm your renewal terms with your broker or carrier.

The announcement sounded like a windfall. The New York State Department of Financial Services approved a 21.9% decrease in workers' compensation loss cost rates, effective October 1, 2026, with the state estimating more than $1 billion in savings for employers, about $1,779 per policyholder on average. It is the eleventh straight year of decreases. If you employ people in New York, you probably saw the headline and did quick math on your own premium.

Before you count the savings, understand what got cut. The state reduced the loss cost, which is the base rate per $100 of payroll for each classification code. Your premium is that base rate multiplied by your payroll, your experience modification, and your carrier's credits or debits. The state cut one input. The other three are still yours.

An average is not a promise

The 21.9% figure is an aggregate average across every class code in the state. The actual change was calculated class by class, and it varies. Some classifications saw bigger decreases. Some saw smaller ones. A few saw increases. Your business lives in specific class codes, not in the average.

So the first question is not "how much did rates drop" but "how much did my class codes drop." Your broker can show you the manual rate change for your classifications at renewal. If your payroll sits mostly in classes that barely moved, the statewide headline does not describe your situation.

The three things that eat the cut

Even in a class code that got the full decrease, three common problems can absorb the savings before they reach your bottom line.

1. A high experience mod

Your mod multiplies everything. A 22% rate cut means the base becomes roughly 78 cents on the dollar. But if your mod is 1.25, you are paying 25% above manual, and 0.78 times 1.25 is 0.975. You are still paying nearly full price. The cut happened. Your claims history ate it.

This is the most common way employers miss out on rate decreases. The mod is driven by your loss history over a three-year experience period, and it moves slowly. One bad year of unmanaged claims can keep your mod elevated long after rates fall. If your mod is above 1.00, fixing it is worth more than any rate filing.

2. Wrong class codes

If your payroll is assigned to the wrong classification, you are paying the wrong rate per $100, and a percentage cut of the wrong number is still the wrong number. Misclassification is quiet and common: job duties drift, new roles get lumped into the nearest code, and nobody rechecks. A 22% cut applied to an inflated base rate still leaves money on the table every payroll cycle.

Class codes deserve a fresh look at every renewal, especially if your operations changed during the year. The rate cut is a good reason to ask your broker to walk through each code on your policy and confirm it still matches the work being done.

3. Bloated reserves on open claims

Reserves are your carrier's estimate of what open claims will ultimately cost, and they feed directly into your mod. When reserves sit higher than the claim warrants, your mod stays higher than it should, for years. The rate cut does nothing about this. It is entirely a function of how closely someone is watching your open files.

Ask your broker or a claims reviewer to walk through your open reserves before renewal. Stale reserves on old claims are one of the most fixable leaks in a comp program, and they are invisible on the renewal quote unless someone goes looking.

What to do before your next renewal

Renewals on or after October 1, 2026 pick up the new rates, so the timing matters now. Four steps will tell you whether the cut is actually reaching you:

  • Get your mod worksheet and confirm it is at or below 1.00. If it is not, find out which policy years and which claims are driving it.
  • Verify your class codes against the actual work your employees do, not what was on the application three years ago.
  • Review open reserves with someone who will challenge them, not just accept the carrier's numbers.
  • Compare the manual rate change to your actual quote. If your class rates fell 20% and your premium fell 3%, the gap is telling you exactly where to look.

One more line item to check: the 2026 employer assessment rate is 7.0% of standard premium, a separate charge that funds the Rating Board. It is small next to the premium itself, but it is worth confirming it is calculated correctly on your bill.

The cut is real. Whether you get it is up to your program.

Lower rates help every employer in the state, and eleven straight years of decreases is genuinely good news. But a rate cut is a tailwind, not a rescue. Employers with clean mods, correct classifications, and well-managed claims will feel the full benefit. Employers with a 1.30 mod and five stale open files will wonder what everyone is celebrating.
