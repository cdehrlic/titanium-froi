A Donkey Escaped. A Carpenter Wrestled It Back. New York Says the Heart Attack Counts.

A carpenter wrestled a runaway donkey at his boss's house, then had a heart attack. On October 8, 2026, New York's Appellate Division upheld his workers' comp claim. The case carries two lessons for employers: odd tasks at the boss's request are still work, and a missed carrier deadline can wipe out every defense.

This article summarizes a published court decision in general terms and is not legal advice.

The facts read like a story, but the holding is pure procedure. A carpenter employed by Moy Construction Corp. was asked to work on a shed at the company owner's home. The shed housed animals. When he arrived, the owner's spouse, who also served as the company's vice president, asked him to help get a loose donkey back inside. They forced it in, the donkey tried to break out again, and the worker grabbed the animal to keep it contained. Soon after, he developed chest pains, lightheadedness, and vomiting. He drove home, and his son brought him to the hospital, where doctors confirmed a heart attack.

The employer's carrier contested the claim. That is where the case was lost. New York's Workers' Compensation Law requires a carrier to file a notice of controversy within 25 days of being notified that a claim has been indexed. Moy's carrier missed that deadline. The miss barred the carrier from arguing there was no employer-employee relationship, no accident, or no injury arising from the employment. A law judge first disallowed the claim because emergency room records appeared to be missing, but on appeal the Workers' Compensation Board found the records were already in the file and established the claim, finding a causal relationship between the heart attack and the employment.

Where the carrier lost it

On October 8, the Appellate Division, Third Department, affirmed the Board on all counts. Because the carrier filed late, the Board had to evaluate the worker's evidence without considering the carrier's proof, and the carrier was barred from disputing causation. The emergency room records noted chest tightness "while exerting himself at work," and the treating physician linked the heart attack to the exertion of wrestling the donkey. The court found that to be sufficient, unrebutted, and credible medical evidence.

The carrier also tried a new argument, that the worker was not covered under its policy. The court would not hear it, because the argument had never been raised below. Arguments surface early or not at all.

Lesson one: odd tasks at the boss's request are still work

When a company officer assigns a worker an unusual task, even wrangling a donkey at the boss's house, an injury from that task can still arise "in the course of employment." Coverage follows the job as assigned, not the job description. Employers who send workers on personal errands for the owner are keeping those workers inside the coverage, whether they intend to or not.

Lesson two: deadlines decide cases before the facts do

The carrier may have had real arguments. It never got to make them because it missed a 25-day deadline. In workers' comp, procedure is not background paperwork. It is the case. A single missed filing turned a defensible claim into an uncontested one.

What to do now

  • Keep assignments work-related. If you need personal help at your home or on a side project, think twice about using your employees. The claim follows the assignment.
  • Document unusual tasks the same day. If an injury happens on an odd assignment, record who assigned it and why, immediately. That record is your evidence about what was actually in the course of employment.
  • Watch your carrier's deadlines. You cannot file the carrier's notices yourself, but you can hold it accountable. Ask who owns each claim, what is due, and when. A carrier that misses a 25-day controversy deadline is not managing your money.
  • Report injuries fast and fully. Early, complete reporting is what lets everyone evaluate defenses while there is still time to use them.

Strange assignments and missed deadlines are the same problem

This case looks funny until you realize it is about claims that are not being managed closely. Unusual assignments create coverage questions nobody documented. A missed deadline erases defenses nobody filed. Both are what a loose claims process looks like.
