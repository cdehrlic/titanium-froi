The First 24 Hours After a Workplace Injury: An Employer's Playbook

Most workers' comp claims are won or lost in the first day. Not in the hearing room, not in the reserve review. In the first 24 hours, while memories are fresh, evidence is intact, and nobody has hired a lawyer yet. Here is exactly what to do, hour by hour.

This is a plain-English playbook for employers on handling the first day after a workplace injury, with New York deadlines called out. It describes general practices and is not legal advice. Reporting rules and procedures can change; confirm current requirements for your jurisdiction.

Ask any experienced claims professional when a workers' comp claim is decided and you will hear some version of the same answer: in the first 24 hours. That is when witness memories are sharpest, the scene is untouched, and the injured worker has not yet spoken to an attorney or heard a coworker suggest the injury could be worth something. Everything you do in that window either builds a defensible file or hands the other side their case.

Most employers waste it. The supervisor means to write the report tomorrow. Nobody takes photos before the spill is cleaned up. The carrier hears about it a week later. Then, months down the road, the claim looks nothing like what actually happened, and there is no paper trail to prove otherwise.

This is the playbook we wish every supervisor carried in their pocket.

First: people, then the scene

None of what follows matters more than this. Get medical help for the injured worker immediately, and do not let paperwork slow that down. Once the person is cared for, secure the scene. Do not clean it up, do not move equipment, do not let the shift reset the area. If the injury is serious, treat the scene the way investigators will wish you had: untouched until it is documented.

If there is any chance OSHA gets involved, know its clock now, not later. A work-related fatality must be reported to OSHA within 8 hours. A hospitalization, amputation, or loss of an eye must be reported within 24 hours. Missing those deadlines brings citations on top of the claim.

Get the supervisor's report done today, not tomorrow

The supervisor who was there, or the first supervisor notified, writes the incident report the same day. Facts only: who was injured, what happened, when, where, what equipment or materials were involved, and who saw it. The injured worker's account goes in their own words, signed and dated. Memory degrades fast. A report written three days later reads like a reconstruction, because it is one, and everyone in the process knows it.

One line supervisors should never write: any statement about fault. No "our fault," no "his fault." Just what happened.

Separate the witnesses and get signed statements now

Talk to each witness alone, immediately. Witnesses who compare notes produce one blended story instead of three honest ones. Get each statement in the witness's own words, signed and dated, with their contact information. If there were no witnesses, write that down too: "no witnesses identified." An empty space in the file invites someone to fill it later. A documented negative closes the question.

Photograph and video everything before it changes

Phones make this free. Photograph the scene, the equipment, the conditions, and the injury if the worker consents. Wide shots for context, close shots for detail. If there is video surveillance, preserve it immediately and note the retention window. Surveillance footage that gets overwritten on day 30 is the most common evidence loss we see, and it is entirely preventable.

Call your carrier today

Do not wait for the paperwork to be perfect. Call your workers' comp carrier or TPA the same day and give them what you have. Prompt notice is usually a policy requirement, and late reporting is one of the cheapest ways to damage your own position. Early notice means the adjuster sets reserves based on real information instead of guessing, starts the investigation while evidence exists, and gets ahead of the claim before the worker's attorney frames it. A carrier that hears about the injury on day one and a carrier that hears about it on day twelve are working two different claims.

Start the C-2F clock (New York)

In New York, the employer's first report of injury, Form C-2F, goes to the Workers' Compensation Board within 10 days of the injury or of learning about it, under Workers' Compensation Law Section 110. Miss the deadline and it is a misdemeanor with a fine, plus the Board can impose a penalty of up to $2,500. If your insurer files the accident information with the Board electronically on your behalf, you generally do not need to file the C-2F separately, but confirm that with your carrier rather than assuming it. Put the 10-day date on a calendar the day the injury happens. Ten days passes faster than anyone expects.

Drug and alcohol testing: only with a written policy

Post-accident testing is a tool, not a reflex. It only helps if you have a written policy, communicated in advance, that requires testing after workplace accidents and you apply it consistently to everyone. Testing one worker and not another, or testing without a policy at all, creates more legal exposure than it prevents. If you do not have a policy, the day after an accident is the wrong time to invent one. Write it now, for the next claim.

What not to do in the first 24 hours

  • Do not admit fault or promise outcomes. "We will take care of everything" becomes an exhibit. Stick to facts and concern for the worker.
  • Do not coach the story. Never suggest what the worker or witnesses should say. One hint of coaching destroys the credibility of every statement in the file.
  • Do not sit on it. Every day of delay is a day the worker talks to coworkers, family, and eventually a lawyer without any facts from you on the record.
  • Do not throw anything away. Damaged equipment, torn clothing, broken tools. Preserve it all until the claim is resolved.

Day two: stay in contact

The first 24 hours end, but the window that matters stays open a little longer. Call the injured worker the next day. Not to interrogate, just to check in. Workers who feel abandoned by their employer in the first week are the ones who hire attorneys. Ask how they are doing, tell them the claim has been reported, and give them a single point of contact for questions. Then start the return-to-work conversation early: what the doctor allows, what light duty looks like, and when you expect them back. The claims with the shortest durations almost always started with contact in the first 48 hours.
