The Doctors Never Knew About the Old Injury. That Is Why This Part of the Claim Failed.

A transit worker with 27 years on the job filed an occupational disease claim for both shoulders, both wrists, and a knee. Most of it was established. The right wrist was not, because every doctor who tied that wrist to his work was missing one basic fact: he had hurt the same wrist at work back in 1997, and the pain from that injury never went away. On November 12, 2026, New York's Appellate Division said the Board was right to set those medical opinions aside.

This article summarizes a published court decision in general terms and is not legal advice.

Wade Miles worked for the New York City Transit Authority from 1994 to 2021, first as a train conductor and then primarily as a construction flagger. In November 2023, two years after he stopped working, he filed a workers' comp claim saying that years of repetitive movement and strain on the job had caused conditions in both of his shoulders, both of his wrists, and his right knee.

A workers' comp judge established the claim as an occupational disease for the shoulders, the left wrist, and the right knee, but disallowed the part of the claim involving the right wrist. The Workers' Compensation Board affirmed that result in a decision filed June 2, 2025. Miles appealed, and his appeal focused on the right wrist.

An occupational disease claim lives or dies on the medical proof

On November 12, 2026, the Appellate Division, Third Department, affirmed the Board in Matter of Miles v. New York City Transit Authority, No. CV-25-0924. The court restated the test. To get benefits for an occupational disease, a worker has to show a recognizable link between the condition and a distinctive feature of the job, and that link has to come through competent medical evidence.

The medical proof also has to clear a quality bar. It must point to a probability that is supported by a rational basis. A general statement that the job could have caused the condition is not enough. And whether a condition counts as an occupational disease is a factual call for the Board, which the court will uphold if substantial evidence in the record supports it, even if the record could also have supported a different result.

The missing fact: a 1997 injury to the same wrist

On the surface, the medical proof for the right wrist looked strong. The worker's treating doctors said there was a causal link between his long years of flagger work and the right wrist condition. So did a doctor who examined him at the request of the Transit Authority itself, which was self-insured.

There was one problem. None of those doctors knew that Miles had injured his right wrist at work in 1997 and had pain after that injury that never went away. Their opinions about what caused the wrist condition were built without that history. The court said the Board was free to reject each of those opinions for exactly that reason, because they were not based on an understanding of his relevant medical treatment history.

Once those opinions were set aside, what was left was what the court called a dearth of credible medical proof tying the right wrist condition to a distinctive feature of his work. On that record, substantial evidence supported the Board's finding that no occupational disease had been established for the right wrist. The decision was affirmed without costs.

Lesson one: history is evidence, and it has to reach the doctors

This case did not turn on a new test or a technicality. It turned on an old injury record. A prior injury to the same body part, with pain that never fully resolved, changes what any honest doctor can say about causation. But that record only helps if it actually gets in front of the doctors offering opinions in the case, including your own examiner. An opinion formed without the full history is an opinion the Board can simply disregard.

Lesson two: these claims are decided body part by body part

Note what did not happen here. The whole claim was not thrown out. The shoulders, left wrist, and right knee were established, and those parts of the claim stand. The right wrist failed on its own proof. Employers sometimes treat a multi-site repetitive strain claim as one fight. It is really several fights, one per body part, and each one needs its own medical record, its own history check, and its own causation argument.

Lesson three: late filed claims make the history check more important, not less

This claim was filed in November 2023 for a worker whose employment ended in 2021, covering a work history that started in 1994. The longer the gap between the work and the claim, the more the case depends on records: old incident reports, prior claim files, and treatment notes going back years. Employers that keep clean, retrievable injury records are the ones who can put that history in front of a doctor when it matters.

What to do now

  • Pull the full prior injury history on every occupational disease claim. Check for earlier injuries to the same body parts, including old comp claims, before any doctor forms an opinion in the case.
  • Make sure the history reaches every examining doctor. That includes your carrier's examiner. An opinion based on an incomplete history can be rejected outright, whichever side it favors.
  • Work each body part separately. In a claim alleging several sites, ask what the medical proof shows for each one, and where the history supports or undercuts causation for that site.
  • Keep old injury records retrievable. A 1997 injury decided part of a claim decided in 2026. Records retention is claims defense, not paperwork.

Good records quietly win claims

Nothing dramatic happened in this case. No fraud, no missed deadline, no waived defense. A worker with a long career got most of his claim established, and one body part failed because the medical opinions behind it were built on an incomplete story. That is how a lot of New York comp claims are actually decided: on whether the file tells the doctor the whole history.
