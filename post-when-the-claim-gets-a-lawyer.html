When the Claim Gets a Lawyer

Two out of three open claims end up with an attorney. The first 24 hours decide whether yours is one of them.

This story is a fictionalized composite based on real claims. Names and identifying details have been changed.

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, five weeks after the injury. The HR director at a New Jersey distribution company opened it between meetings and felt her stomach drop. A law firm was now representing the warehouse associate who had hurt his knee on the loading dock last month. From this point forward, the letter said, all communication would go through counsel.

Nothing about the injury itself had changed. What had changed was everything the company didn't do in the five weeks before that letter arrived.

Five weeks of silence

The injury was ordinary. The associate twisted his knee stepping off a loading dock. He reported it to his supervisor the same day, filled out the incident form, and went home. The supervisor filed the form in a drawer and told him to take it easy. Nobody called him that week. Nobody called him the next week either. His doctor put him on light duty, but nobody at the company followed up, because nobody was assigned to follow up.

In week three, a neighbor asked how the knee was doing. The neighbor knew a workers' comp lawyer. "You know the company isn't going to take care of you, right?" By week four, the associate had stopped expecting the phone to ring. In week five, it rang. It was the lawyer's office, confirming the consultation.

This is the pattern behind most represented claims. It is rarely about how bad the injury is. It is about silence. An injured worker who feels invisible starts looking for someone who will pay attention. There is an entire industry happy to be that someone.

What the lawyer changed

The day counsel got involved, the economics of the claim changed completely.

Direct communication ended. Every question now traveled through two law offices. Simple things, like confirming a doctor's appointment or offering light duty, took weeks instead of hours.

The timeline stretched. Hearings were scheduled, adjourned, and rescheduled. A claim that might have closed in three months was now measured in years.

And the leverage shifted. With an attorney driving, every decision the carrier made was weighed against litigation risk. Reserves went up, not because the knee got worse, but because the file got a lawyer. When settlement finally came, it cost multiples of what early management would have cost.

None of that was the lawyer's fault. The lawyer did exactly what lawyers do. The fault was the five weeks of silence that made the lawyer necessary.

The first 24 hours

Now compare that to the claims that never get a lawyer. They are not luckier claims. They are managed claims, and the management starts on day one:

  • Call the injured worker within 24 hours. Not to interrogate. To ask how they are doing and explain what happens next.
  • Document the facts while they are fresh. What happened, where, who saw it. Memories degrade fast.
  • Offer light duty in writing, immediately, if the doctor allows any work at all. An employee back at work, even on restricted duty, is an employee who doesn't feel abandoned.
  • Stay in contact. A two-minute call each week costs nothing and keeps the neighbor's lawyer out of the picture.

None of this requires a big company or a risk management department. It requires one person who owns the first 24 hours and doesn't let go.

The lesson for employers

On any given day, the majority of open claim files on an adjuster's desk have attorneys attached. That is not a legal problem. It is a management problem that became a legal problem.

You cannot control whether an employee gets hurt. You can control whether they feel taken care of afterward. The employers with the fewest represented claims are not the ones with the fewest injuries. They are the ones whose injured workers never had a reason to call a lawyer.
