The Contractor Test: 5 Questions That Decide Your Workers' Comp Exposure

You call them contractors. You pay them on 1099s. Everyone signed the agreement. None of that decides the question. When a state auditor or workers' comp board looks at the relationship, they run their own test, and if your workers fail it, the bill comes with back premium, interest, and penalties. Here are the five questions they ask, and the paperwork that answers them.

The most expensive sentence in a workers' comp audit is "we treat everyone as a contractor." Auditors hear it constantly. It has never once decided the outcome. What decides it is the working relationship itself: who tells the worker what to do, who supplies the tools, who bears the risk if the job goes sideways.

This matters more every year. Staffing platforms, gig arrangements, and long-term 1099 relationships are everywhere in construction, healthcare, delivery, and facilities work. States know it, and misclassification enforcement keeps growing. We covered a real case file on this theme in our Claim Files series, where a staffing platform learned the hard way that its "contractors" were employees in the eyes of the board. This guide is the practical version: the test, the questions, and the file you should be keeping.

The test is about the relationship, not the label

Every state has its own version of the employee-versus-contractor test for workers' comp, and the details differ. But the underlying logic is consistent everywhere: a true independent contractor is running their own business. An employee is working in yours. The questions below are the ones auditors, boards, and carriers keep coming back to. No single question decides it alone. They weigh the whole picture.

Question 1: Who controls how the work gets done?

This is the heavyweight. Control means the right to direct not just what gets done, but how, when, and where. Do you set the schedule? Do you require them to be at your site at specific hours? Do you supervise the work as it happens, or do you just inspect the finished result?

A contractor you hired to repaint the office decides when to show up, brings their own crew, and you judge the finished paint job. A "contractor" who clocks in at your warehouse at 8 a.m., takes assignments from your supervisor, and follows your procedures is an employee wearing a 1099 costume. The more day-to-day control you exercise, the harder the contractor label is to defend.

Question 2: Does the worker run their own business?

Real contractors have businesses. They have a business name, their own clients, their own marketing, their own insurance. Ask yourself: if you stopped calling tomorrow, would this person still have work? If the answer is no, that is a problem.

Auditors look for the markers of independence. Multiple clients is the strongest one. A worker who does similar jobs for three different companies looks like a contractor. A worker who has done nothing but your jobs for two years looks like your employee. Business cards, a website, and a separately filed business entity all help, but the client list is what carries weight.

Question 3: Who provides the tools, and who bears the financial risk?

Contractors invest in their own work. They buy their own equipment, maintain their own vehicles, and eat the cost when a job runs long. Employees show up and use your stuff.

This question has two halves. First, the tools: if you hand the worker a company truck, a company laptop, or a company uniform, you are supplying the means of production, which points toward employment. Second, the risk: a contractor who bids a fixed price and loses money when the job takes twice as long is bearing real business risk. A worker paid by the hour with no downside looks like an employee. Guaranteed hourly pay with your equipment is the classic misclassification setup.

Question 4: Do they work only for you?

Exclusivity is a quiet killer. A contractor is free to take other work, and real ones do. If your agreement forbids outside clients, or if the workload simply leaves no room for any, the relationship starts to look exclusive in fact even if the contract says otherwise.

Watch for the long-term exclusive 1099. It is the most common pattern auditors flag: the same person, paid on a 1099, doing the same role, for months or years, with no other clients. Duration plus exclusivity is the combination that sinks the most contractor defenses. Short project engagements with defined end dates are far easier to defend than open-ended arrangements that renew forever.

Question 5: Is the work part of your core business?

This one surprises people. If you run a plumbing company and you hire a plumber on a 1099, auditors will ask why your "contractor" does exactly what your business does. Work that is integral to your core operation points toward employment. Work that is outside it points the other way.

The plumber hiring an accountant on a 1099 is on solid ground. The plumbing company staffing its service calls with 1099 plumbers is not. Staffing firms and platforms sit right in the danger zone here, because the workers they place are doing the exact work the client sells. If your contractors do what you do, you need the other four answers to be airtight.

What happens when the state disagrees

When an auditor or board reclassifies your contractors as employees, three things happen, roughly in this order. First, back premium: your carrier recalculates what you should have paid, going back over the audit period, and bills you for the unreported payroll. Second, penalties and interest, which vary by state but are never small. Third, ongoing exposure: those workers are now on your policy going forward, at your rates, with your mod.

And the workers' comp bill is often just the start. The same reclassification can trigger unemployment insurance assessments, tax consequences, and wage claims. The comp audit is usually where employers first discover the problem, because the payroll audit is where the paper trail gets examined line by line.

The file that protects you: a documentation checklist

You cannot control which test your state applies, but you can control the evidence. For every contractor relationship, keep a file with these items, current and signed:

  • A written independent contractor agreement that describes the scope of work, the project or term, payment terms, and each side's responsibilities. Signed by both parties, dated, and specific to the actual work.
  • Proof of the contractor's business: business registration or DBA filing, business cards or website, evidence of other clients.
  • Certificates of insurance showing the contractor carries their own workers' comp (or a valid exemption where the state allows it) and general liability. Collect them before work starts and track expiration dates. An expired COI is the same as no COI at an audit.
  • Invoices, not timesheets. Contractors bill you. Employees submit hours. Keep the invoices, and make sure they describe completed work or milestones, not blocks of time.
  • Project-based payment records showing fixed-price or per-project terms rather than ongoing hourly wages.
  • 1099s filed correctly and on time for every contractor paid above the reporting threshold.

One warning about the agreement itself: a signed contract helps, but it does not override reality. Auditors read the contract, then they look at how the work actually happened. If the paperwork says "independent contractor" and the supervisor's texts say "be here at 8 a.m. sharp," the texts win.

Staffing and gig arrangements need extra attention

If you use a staffing firm or a labor platform, get clarity in writing about who carries the workers' comp. In a proper staffing arrangement, the staffing firm is the employer of record and insures the workers. Get the certificate and confirm it is current. But do not assume the paperwork settles it: if your supervisors direct the placed workers day to day, some states will still look at you. The contract between you and the platform should spell out who insures whom, who supervises, and what happens if there is a claim.

Do the review before the auditor does

Pull your 1099 list from last year and run each relationship through the five questions honestly. Flag the long-term exclusives, the hourly-paid "contractors" using your equipment, and anyone doing your core work. Those are your highest-risk files. Fix the documentation now, restructure the relationship where you can, and talk to your broker about whether your current policy reflects reality.

This is also worth doing before renewal. Unreported contractor payroll discovered at a premium audit becomes back premium with interest. The same payroll disclosed and classified correctly up front is just premium. The difference between those two outcomes is timing and paperwork.
