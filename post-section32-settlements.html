Should You Settle That Claim? A Section 32 Decision Guide for New York Employers

The claimant's lawyer wants a settlement. That does not mean you should want one too. In New York, a Section 32 agreement closes a workers' comp claim permanently, and the math cuts both ways. Here is how to tell a good settlement from an expensive one, and how your reserves set your leverage before you ever sit down.

This article discusses New York workers' compensation settlements in general strategic terms. It is not legal advice. Settlement decisions involve legal rights and should be reviewed with your counsel before you agree to anything.

If you employ people in New York, you will eventually get the call. The claimant's attorney wants to talk settlement. A Section 32 agreement, full and final, closes the claim: no more weekly checks, no more medical, no more hearings. Everyone walks away. It sounds like relief, and sometimes it is. But the claimant's lawyer pushes settlements because settlements pay the lawyer. Your reasons for settling should be your own, and they should be built on math, not on wanting the file to go away.

This guide is the employer's side of that decision: what a Section 32 actually does to your costs, when closing a claim helps your experience mod, when you are paying extra for peace of mind, and how to walk into the negotiation with leverage instead of hope.

What a Section 32 actually does

Section 32 of the New York Workers' Compensation Law lets the parties settle a claim by agreement, subject to approval by a Workers' Compensation Law Judge. Once approved, the settlement is final. The carrier pays the agreed lump sum, and the claim closes on both indemnity (lost wage benefits) and medical, unless the parties specifically carve medical out, which is rare and usually unwise from the employer's perspective.

Finality is the whole point. An open claim is a living thing: reserves move, treatment continues, hearings get scheduled, and every development lands on your loss history. A settled claim becomes a fixed number. For an employer, that certainty has real value. The question is always what you are paying for it.

When settling helps your mod

Your experience mod is driven by actual losses versus expected losses over a three-year window. An open claim with high reserves hurts you every year it stays open, because those reserves count as losses. Settling can help in three specific ways.

First, it caps the number. If the reserve on a file is $180,000 and you settle for $120,000, the lower figure is what flows into your loss history going forward. The $60,000 gap is real money off your mod.

Second, it stops the bleed. Open claims accumulate: ongoing treatment, new diagnostic work, attorney-driven development. Every additional dollar of paid or reserved loss extends the damage. A settlement freezes the file where it stands.

Third, it removes volatility. One catastrophic reserve spike in a single policy year can distort your mod for three years. Employers with thin payrolls feel this hardest, because one big claim dominates their expected losses. Closing the file converts an unknown into a known.

The pattern: settling helps most when reserves are high relative to what the claim is actually worth, when the file keeps growing, or when a single claim is swinging your mod. In those cases, the settlement is not just peace of mind. It is arithmetic.

When you are overpaying for peace

Now the other side. Claimant attorneys are professionals at making settlement feel urgent, and some employers pay a premium just to stop thinking about a file. Here are the situations where the settlement number is working against you.

The claim is defensible and you have not tested it. If causation is shaky, the medical does not support ongoing disability, or an independent medical exam would likely reduce exposure, settling before developing that evidence means paying for a claim you might have beaten or shrunk. Defense costs money too, but so does surrender.

The demand prices in medical care that will never happen. Settlement demands routinely include projections of future treatment: surgeries, injections, therapy, lifetime prescriptions. Some of that is real. Much of it is aspirational. If the treating history shows conservative care working, or the worker has already returned to full duty, a demand built on worst-case medical is a number you should challenge, not accept.

You are settling out of fatigue, not strategy. The most expensive settlements we see are not the biggest ones. They are the ones where the employer was simply tired of the file: tired of the hearings, tired of the attorney letters, tired of explaining the reserve to the CFO. Fatigue is a real cost, but it should be priced honestly. If the only reason to settle is exhaustion, get a second set of eyes on the file first. You may find the claim is closer to resolution than it feels.

The timing hands the claimant leverage. Settling right before a hearing where the carrier is likely to win, or right after a bad IME for the claimant that has not been leveraged yet, means paying for momentum the other side does not actually have. The calendar matters. We cover that below.

Reserves are your leverage

Here is the part most employers miss: the reserve is the starting point of every settlement negotiation, and you have more influence over it than you think.

The carrier sets reserves, but the carrier sets them based on the file as it understands it. If the file is thin, if no one has challenged the treatment plan, if the IME was never ordered, the reserve reflects the claimant's version of the claim at its most expensive. Every settlement demand is then anchored to that inflated number. You negotiate down from a ceiling you helped create.

The move is to get the reserve right before settlement talks start. That means reviewing the open file the way the other side's attorney does: what is the actual permanency exposure, what future medical is genuinely likely, what would an IME say today. When the reserve reflects reality, the negotiation starts from reality. Employers who do this work routinely settle for less than employers who let the carrier's number stand unexamined.

There is a second leverage point most employers never use: information. The claimant's attorney knows exactly what the exposure is. Most employers do not. Walking into a settlement discussion without your own independent read of the file's value is negotiating blind. A claim review that prices the file, the way we do it, turns the conversation from "what will you take" into "here is what it is worth."

Timing matters more than you think

When you settle changes what you pay. A few timing principles from the employer's chair:

  • After maximum medical improvement, not before. Settling before MMI means pricing unknown medical. Once the treating doctor or an IME establishes MMI and a permanency schedule, the future medical component becomes calculable instead of speculative. Speculative is always more expensive.
  • After your IME, not before. An independent medical exam that finds reduced disability or no causal relationship is the single strongest settlement lever an employer has. Settling before the IME report lands means paying the claimant's medical narrative at face value.
  • Before the next reserve increase, if one is coming. If the file is about to get worse, a surgery recommendation, a new body part added, prolonged lost time, settling before the reserve jumps can lock in the lower number. This is the mirror image of the fatigue trap: speed helps when the trajectory is bad, hurts when it is good.
  • With an eye on your mod window. Because the mod looks back three years, the timing of when a settlement hits your loss history matters. Your broker or a claims reviewer can model how the settlement amount lands across your experience period. Sometimes waiting a quarter changes the math.

The settle-or-hold checklist

Run every settlement opportunity through these questions before you decide:

  • Is the reserve accurate? Has someone independent reviewed it against the actual medical and permanency exposure, or are you negotiating from the carrier's unexamined number?
  • Is the medical story complete? Has MMI been reached? Is there a current IME? Are you pricing real future treatment or the claimant's wish list?
  • What happens if you do nothing? Price the hold option: projected paid losses, reserve trajectory, and mod impact over the next two to three years. Sometimes the file resolves itself cheaper than any settlement.
  • What is the claimant's urgency? A claimant pushing hard for settlement may have reasons, financial pressure, a weak upcoming hearing, an IME they fear. Urgency on their side is leverage on yours.
  • Does the number beat your walk-away? Set your number before the discussion, based on the file's real value, not on how tired you are of it. If the demand does not beat it, hold.
  • Have you talked to counsel? Section 32 settlements extinguish legal rights permanently and require judge approval. Your attorney should review the terms before you agree to anything.

A good settlement vs a bad one

A good settlement, from the employer's chair, has three features: the number is at or below the file's independently assessed value, it closes medical as well as indemnity, and it lands at a point in the claim lifecycle where the unknowns are resolved rather than priced in. You walk away knowing exactly what the claim cost and why.

A bad settlement is one where you paid for the claimant's version of the file: unexamined reserves, unchallenged medical projections, and timing that favored the other side. The tell is regret. If you would feel uneasy explaining the number to your CFO with the file open on the table, the number was probably wrong.

The claimant's bar will always push settlements, because that is how their business works. Your job is simpler: settle when the math says settle, hold when it says hold, and never confuse wanting a file closed with having a reason to close it. Talk to your counsel, price the file independently, and make the decision like an owner, not a hostage.
