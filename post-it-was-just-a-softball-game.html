It Was Just a Softball Game

The carrier called it voluntary recreation and denied the claim. That was only the first fight. The second was over whose employee he was.

This story is a fictionalized composite based on real claims. Names and identifying details have been changed.

The collision happened in the outfield, during the part of the year that was supposed to be fun. A rehab manager went back on a fly ball. A player from the other facility came in on the same ball from the opposite direction. Neither of them heard the other call for it. The manager did not get up.

The injuries were not the kind anyone shakes off. He was knocked unconscious, with a brain bleed, a concussion, and six facial fractures. He spent time in intensive care. By the time the claim paperwork caught up with the hospital paperwork, the carrier had already reached its conclusion: denied. This was a voluntary recreational activity, off duty, unpaid, and outside the course of employment.

Notice what the denial did not say. It did not say whose claim it would have been if it had been covered. That turned out to be the harder question.

A game the company built

The game was not something a few employees cooked up on their own. It was the annual inter-facility softball game, a standing tradition between sister rehab facilities. Management chose the date. Management reserved the field, bought the team shirts, and paid for the food. A sign-up sheet lived in the break room. Supervisors mentioned the game in huddles and encouraged people to come out. The rosters were posted where everyone could see them.

On paper, attendance was voluntary. Nobody was written up for staying home. But the event existed because the company built it, promoted it, and funded it. It was the facility's game in every way except one line in the file that said nobody had to go.

That one line carried the whole denial.

Why the carrier said no, and why that is not the end of it

In many states, injuries during employer-sponsored recreation are treated differently from injuries on the job. When an activity is genuinely voluntary, off the premises, off the clock, and unpaid, a carrier has a real argument that the injury did not arise out of employment. Three of those facts were right there in this file, and the denial wrote itself from them.

But boards rarely stop at the label on the activity. They look at the connection between the event and the employer. Who organized it. Who paid for it. Whether attendance was expected in practice, even if it was optional on paper. And what the employer got back. Companies do not fund annual games out of pure generosity. In a short-staffed healthcare operation, events like this are retention tools. They build the teamwork that sister facilities rely on when they share staff and cover each other's gaps. A benefit that real is part of the analysis, not a footnote to it.

The rules vary by state, and the same game can come out differently on opposite sides of a state line. The point is narrower and more uncomfortable: "voluntary recreation" is an argument, not a verdict. It is strongest when the employer's fingerprints are barely on the event. Here, the fingerprints were everywhere.

Then the second fight started

The injured manager had worked at the facility full-time for well over a year. The facility set his schedule. Facility supervisors directed his work and evaluated him. He was trained on the facility's protocols and wore its name on his shirt at the game. But his paycheck, and his W-2, came from a staffing agency. On paper, the agency was his employer. In daily life, the facility was.

So once the recreation argument was on the table, a second argument stood up behind it. The staffing agency's carrier pointed at the facility: it controlled the man's work, and the game was its event. The facility's carrier pointed back at the agency: he was their employee on every document that mattered, and in any case the claim was a recreation denial. Two carriers, two reasons to say no, and one man recovering from a head injury while the file went back and forth.

Workers' comp law has a name for this arrangement. When a worker is supplied by one business but controlled by another, the controlling business can be treated as a special employer, with real responsibility for the claim, alongside or instead of the general employer on the paperwork. The test is control, and control is a facts question. That means the second fight can take longer to resolve than the first one, and longer than the injury takes to heal.

Where it stands

The matter is still being worked, and both questions are being fought at the same time: whether a company-built game is covered recreation, and which employer's coverage answers if it is. That is the real lesson of this file. The question everyone argued about first, the softball question, may not even be the question that decides who pays. The agency question was sitting underneath it the whole time.

What employers should take from this

  • If your name is on the event, assume a board will connect it to employment. Organizing, funding, and promoting an activity is what connection looks like. The more successful your event is as a company event, the weaker "it had nothing to do with work" becomes.
  • Set the ground rules before the season, in writing. Voluntary or expected. On the clock or off. On premises or off. Write it down, and then run the event the way the paper says. A policy the event itself contradicts is worse than no policy.
  • Ask your carrier about sponsored recreation before anyone gets hurt. How recreational injuries are treated depends on your state and your program. You want that answer in the file before the game, not in a denial letter after it.
  • For agency staff, settle the employer question in the contract. Who carries the comp coverage, who indemnifies whom, and what happens when an agency worker is hurt at a host-run event. If the contract is silent, the carriers will argue it out while the claim ages, and the worker sits in the middle.
  • Do not let two carriers pointing at each other become the worker's problem. One coordinator, one file, one version of the facts. A seriously injured worker left in limbo does not stay patient. That is how defended claims turn into litigated ones.

The lesson for employers

An injury at work is not always covered, and an injury away from work is not always outside the system. The line is not the location. It is the connection: who created the activity, who benefited from it, and who controlled the worker when it happened. If you sponsored the game, you have already answered part of that question yourself. And if the worker came to you through an agency, "he is not really our employee" is a sentence a board gets to test, not a fact you get to declare.
