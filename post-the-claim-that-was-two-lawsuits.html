The Claim That Was Secretly Two Lawsuits

A shoulder injury on a New York construction site, reserves climbing past $100,000, and a second lawsuit nobody saw coming.

This story is a fictionalized composite based on real claims. Names and identifying details have been changed.

The workers' comp claim looked straightforward. A laborer on a Manhattan renovation project fell from a ladder and injured his shoulder. Surgery was recommended. The claim was accepted, treatment authorized, reserves set. Standard file.

Then the reserves kept climbing. Past $50,000. Past $75,000. Past $100,000. The shoulder wasn't getting worse. The number was.

The number that didn't make sense

The employer's broker flagged it at renewal. Nothing in the medicals justified a six-figure reserve on a shoulder claim. The adjuster's explanation was vague: "potential exposure." When pressed, the adjuster finally said what nobody had put in writing: there was a related civil action.

A second envelope had arrived months earlier, and it had never made it into the comp conversation.

The second envelope

Under New York's Labor Law, specifically Section 240, owners and general contractors face strict liability when a worker is injured in a fall from a height. The injured laborer had filed a civil suit against the building owner. Same fall, same shoulder, second lawsuit, running on a parallel track with far larger dollars at stake.

This is routine in New York construction, and it is routinely mishandled. The comp claim and the civil case are handled by different lawyers, different adjusters, different files. Nobody connects them until the numbers force the issue.

Why it matters

Here is why an employer should care about a lawsuit filed against the building owner: everything in the comp file feeds the civil case. Every statement the worker gives, every medical record, every recorded description of how the fall happened becomes evidence in a case worth many times the comp claim. A casual inconsistency in the comp file becomes a weapon in the civil file.

And the reserve reflects total exposure, not just medical bills. When the carrier sees a parallel Labor Law action, it reserves for the worst version of the story. That is why the number kept climbing while the shoulder stayed the same.

The lesson for employers

In New York construction, a fall from height is never just a comp claim. Assume the second case is coming and act like it from day one:

  • Preserve everything: the ladder, photos of the scene, witness names, equipment maintenance records.
  • Coordinate the defense. The comp attorney and the civil defense need to be working from the same facts, not discovering each other at renewal.
  • Take the worker's recorded statements seriously. What gets said casually in the comp file gets read carefully in the civil file.
  • Watch the reserves and ask what they are actually for. "Potential exposure" is not an answer.

The employers who get blindsided by the second lawsuit are the ones who treated the first claim as routine. On a New York job site, there is no such thing as a routine fall.

The paper trail problem

Most employers don't realize how promiscuous claim paperwork is. The first report of injury, the supervisor's incident notes, the recorded statement the adjuster takes in the first week, all of it is discoverable in the civil case. In this file, the worker's description of the ladder's condition shifted slightly between the comp statement and the civil deposition. The plaintiff's attorney in the Labor Law case used the earlier version to attack his credibility, and the defense spent real money cleaning it up.

This is why the first investigation matters twice as much in construction. Get the facts right the first time, in writing, with witnesses, before anyone has a reason to shade them. A clean, consistent record in the comp file is a gift to your civil defense. A sloppy one is a gift to the other side.

It is also worth asking your broker a direct question at every renewal: which of our open claims have related civil actions, and what are we reserved for on each? If your broker can't answer that, you have a visibility problem that goes beyond any single claim. The employers who manage this well treat the comp file and the civil file as one matter with two fronts, because that is what it is.
