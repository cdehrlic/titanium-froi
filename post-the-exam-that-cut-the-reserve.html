The Exam That Cut the Reserve

Eighteen months of treatment, six-figure reserves, and a carrier that never suggested the one exam that ended it.

This story is a fictionalized composite based on real claims. Names and identifying details have been changed.

The claim file told a boring story. A warehouse associate in Pennsylvania strained his back lifting a pallet. Legitimate injury, accepted claim, conservative treatment. Physical therapy, then more physical therapy, then injections. Eighteen months in, he was still treating, still out of work, and the reserves were sitting in six figures.

Nothing about the file was dramatic. That was the problem. It was so routine that nobody questioned it.

The plateau nobody questioned

Every few months the adjuster would note that the claimant was continuing treatment and move on. The treating doctor kept finding reasons to continue: one more round of therapy, one more injection, one more re-evaluation in eight weeks. Each note was reasonable on its own. Together they described a patient who wasn't getting better and a claim that wasn't moving.

The employer watched the reserve number climb at every renewal and assumed there was nothing to be done. The injury was real. The treatment was prescribed by a doctor. What was there to question?

Plenty, as it turned out.

The question that changed everything

At a routine claim review, the employer's operations manager asked the adjuster a simple question: "Has anyone examined him independently? Like, not his doctor?"

There was a pause. Then the adjuster admitted that no independent medical exam had ever been requested. Not once in eighteen months.

An IME was scheduled. It is worth understanding what an IME is and isn't. It isn't an accusation of fraud. It is a second opinion from a doctor with no treatment relationship, whose job is to answer objective questions: What is the diagnosis? Is the treatment working? Has the patient reached maximum medical improvement? Can he work, and with what restrictions?

What the exam found

The IME doctor reviewed eighteen months of records, examined the worker, and wrote the report that should have been written a year earlier. The worker had reached maximum medical improvement months ago. The ongoing treatment wasn't producing functional gains. He could work with permanent restrictions, which the employer could accommodate.

Within weeks of that report, the reserves were cut by more than half. The claim that had been drifting toward a massive settlement was suddenly a manageable file with a defined endpoint. Treatment wound down. A return to work was scheduled. The case settled for a fraction of the reserved amount.

The lesson for employers

Here is the uncomfortable truth: the carrier will almost never volunteer an IME. Every month of treatment is a month of medical bills the carrier processes and passes through in your premium. There is no financial incentive for them to be the one who says enough.

That means the employer has to be the one who asks. Watch for the signs: treatment with no measurable functional improvement, the same therapy repeating without progress, a doctor who never discusses work capacity. Any of those is reason to request an independent look.

An IME doesn't deny legitimate care. It answers the question the file has been avoiding: is this treatment actually helping, or is the claim just drifting? On this file, one exam erased more reserve dollars than a year of hoping the claim would resolve itself.

How to ask for one

Employers often assume they can't request an IME, that it's the carrier's call. In most states the employer, as the party paying the premium, can absolutely push for one through the adjuster, and a good broker will back you up. The key is timing and framing. Don't wait eighteen months like this employer did. Ask at the first claim review where the treatment plan has no defined endpoint.

Frame it as case management, not suspicion: "We want to make sure the treatment plan is working and understand his work capacity." That language gets cooperation. Accusations get resistance. And document the request in writing. If the adjuster says no, ask them to note the file with the reason. Adjusters who have to write down why they refused an independent look tend to reconsider.

The broader point is about who manages your claims. The carrier processes thousands of files. Yours is one of them. Nobody will ever care about your reserve dollars the way you do, which means the oversight has to come from you. One pointed question at one claim review changed the entire trajectory of this file. Make it a habit on every file that stops moving.
