The Carrier Filed on Time. It Forgot to Send the Form to the Other Side. That Cost Every Defense.

A worker slipped at work and hurt his lower back. The carrier disputed the claim and filed its pre-hearing conference statement on time, listing several defenses. It never served that statement on the worker's lawyer. On October 29, 2026, New York's Appellate Division said that one missed step waived the defenses, even though the form was sitting in the electronic case file for everyone to see.

This article summarizes a published court decision in general terms and is not legal advice.

Jason McDonell filed a workers' comp claim in December 2023 after a slip at work that injured his lower back. The employer, CJ Banach Inc., and its carrier disputed the claim. The Workers' Compensation Board then scheduled a pre-hearing conference, the early hearing where each side is supposed to lay out the issues in dispute.

Ten days before that conference, both sides filed their pre-hearing conference statements. The worker's lawyer filed and served his statement on the carrier. The carrier's lawyer filed a statement raising several potential defenses, but did not serve it on the worker's lawyer. At the conference, the worker argued the carrier's defenses were therefore waived.

The carrier acknowledged the error. It said the mistake came from confusion about the name of the worker's lawyer's firm, and it argued the statement was still timely filed and visible to all parties in the electronic case file. The workers' comp judge was not persuaded. The judge ruled the defenses were waived because the statement had not been served, and the case continued on that basis. The Board upheld the judge in a decision filed March 26, 2025, and later denied reconsideration and full Board review on July 9, 2025.

The rule leaves no room to look past a missed service

On October 29, 2026, the Appellate Division, Third Department, affirmed the Board in Matter of McDonell v. CJ Banach Inc., No. CV-25-0750. The court pointed to Workers' Compensation Law section 25(2-a) and the Board's rules, 12 NYCRR 300.33(d) and 300.38(f)(1). Ten days before a pre-hearing conference, each party must file a conference statement noting the specific issues in dispute, and the carrier or its lawyer must file that statement with the Board and serve it on all other parties.

The rule then says what happens if the carrier does not do that. Failure by the carrier to timely serve the pre-hearing conference statement on all other parties shall result in a waiver of defenses to the claim. The court stressed that the language is mandatory. Unlike other Board hearing rules that give a judge discretion or create exceptions, this rule does not. The Board had no discretion to disregard the missed service.

"No harm done" is not an excuse here

The carrier's strongest practical argument was that nobody was actually prejudiced. The statement was filed on time and visible in the electronic case file, so the worker's side could have read it. The court rejected that argument outright. Lack of prejudice does not excuse a failure to serve under this rule.

There is only one way out, and the carrier did not use it. The rule provides that there is no waiver if the judge finds, based on an affidavit from the carrier's lawyer, that the conduct was due to good cause and that the carrier exercised good faith and due diligence. No such affidavit was provided in this case. Without it, the court said, there was no reason to disturb the Board's finding that the defenses were waived. The carrier also tried to argue substantial compliance, but the court said that argument fails for the same reason.

Lesson one: filing is not serving

This is the distinction that decided the case. Putting a document in the electronic case file is filing. Serving it means getting it to the other side's lawyer. A carrier can do the first part perfectly and still lose everything by missing the second part. Employers should not assume that because something is in the file, the other side has been served. Ask for proof of service, not just proof of filing.

Lesson two: the only cure is an affidavit, filed promptly

If service is missed, the rule gives a narrow path back: an affidavit showing good cause, good faith, and due diligence. That is a specific showing, not a general explanation at the conference. Confusion about a firm's name, without an affidavit, did not meet it. When a service error is spotted, the fix has to be documented in the form the rule requires, right away.

Lesson three: procedure decides cases before the facts do

Nothing in this ruling says the carrier's defenses were weak on the merits. The carrier never got to argue them. That is a pattern New York employers have seen before: a missed 25-day notice of controversy deadline can wipe out defenses, and now a missed service step on a pre-hearing statement can do the same. In a controverted claim, the early paperwork is not background work. It is the case.

What to do now

  • Check service, not just filing, on every controverted claim. Confirm the pre-hearing conference statement was served on the claimant's lawyer at least 10 days before the conference, and keep the proof with the file.
  • Verify the other side's counsel details early. A wrong firm name is exactly the kind of small error that caused this waiver. Confirm names and addresses before the deadline, not after.
  • If service is missed, act immediately. The rule's only excuse runs through an affidavit showing good cause, good faith, and due diligence. Raise it promptly and in writing.
  • Hold your carrier to its deadlines. You cannot file the carrier's papers yourself, but you can ask who owns each claim, what is due, and when. A carrier that misses a service step is not protecting your money.

Small service errors carry large claim costs

This claim started with a slip and a back injury. It became a lesson about a form that was filed but never sent. The employer and carrier lost the chance to contest the claim, not because the facts were against them, but because a required service step was skipped and the one cure the rule allows was never offered.
