The $3,000 Penalty

The judge believed the worker and established the claim. Then the judge fined the employer $3,000 for fighting it, about ten times the usual penalty, and the carrier walked away from the appeal.

This story is a fictionalized composite based on real claims. Names and identifying details have been changed.

Nine feet is not a long fall, but it is long enough. An electrician came off a ladder on a commercial job and hurt his back, and the claim that followed was controverted, which is the workers' comp word for the employer and carrier deciding to fight it. The reserves climbed past $100,000. The case went to a hearing.

The judge believed the worker. The claim was established, meaning the injury was accepted as work-related and benefits would be paid. That happens in controverted cases all the time, and by itself it would have been an ordinary loss.

Then the judge added a line that employers almost never see coming. A penalty of $3,000 against the employer for frivolous controversy.

The fine on top of the loss

New York lets a judge penalize a party that controverts a claim without a genuine basis. In practice those penalties are usually a few hundred dollars, more of a judicial slap on the wrist than a financial event. Three thousand dollars is roughly ten times that. It is a judge saying, in writing, that this fight should not have happened.

Understand what the employer bought with that penalty. The claim was established anyway, so the benefits get paid either way. The defense costs were spent either way. The penalty is pure addition, a fine for the quality of the fight itself. And it lands on the employer, the party whose name is on the policy and whose mod absorbs the claim.

Then came the argument over whose fine it was

Here is the part that should make every employer read it twice. The decision to controvert, and the way the controversy was run, usually sit with the carrier and its counsel. They evaluate the evidence, they frame the defense, they decide what to contest. The employer is often a bystander in its own case.

In this file, the carrier declined to appeal the establishment of the claim and left the appeal of the penalty to the employer. The technical argument waiting in that appeal is an interesting one: that a penalty for a frivolous controversy belongs on the party that created the controversy, which points at the carrier or its lawyers, not at the employer who took their advice.

Maybe that argument wins. Maybe it does not. Either way, the employer is now funding an appeal to move a fine off its own books that it did not obviously earn, arising from a strategy it did not obviously choose.

Controversy is a tool, not a reflex

None of this means employers should never fight a claim. Some claims deserve a fight, and a well-run controversy saves employers enormous money. The lesson is narrower and more practical:

  • Ask what the controversy rests on before it is filed. A specific factual dispute, a real medical conflict, a documented inconsistency. If the answer is a general feeling that the claim seems off, that is the profile of a penalty.
  • Ask who decided, and who pays if the judge disagrees. Get the defense strategy on a high-exposure claim in writing, including who bears a frivolous-controversy penalty if one is assessed.
  • Revisit the fight when the evidence moves. A controversy that was reasonable in month one can become unreasonable by month six. Judges judge the whole course of the fight, not just the day it started.
  • Remember who the judge is watching. The employer's name is on the case. Credibility spent on a weak fight is not available later, on the claim that truly deserves one.

The expensive sentence in this file was never the establishment of the claim. Claims get established. It was the judge's conclusion that the fight itself had no business happening, priced at $3,000, and mailed to the one party who may have had the least to do with starting it.

Before your next controverted claim gets rolling, it is worth one phone call. Ask the adjuster two questions: what exactly are we disputing, and if the judge calls it frivolous, whose check pays the fine? The answers tell you whether you have a defense, or just a habit.
