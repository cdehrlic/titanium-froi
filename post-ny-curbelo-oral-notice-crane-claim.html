He Never Put It in Writing. The Notice Defense Still Failed.

A crane caught fire and collapsed through the ceiling of the building where Gonzalo Curbelo was working as a foreperson. He told his supervisor at the scene that his crew was safe and that he had been hurt by falling debris. He never gave timely written notice of the injury. On November 25, 2026, New York's Appellate Division still affirmed his established claim, because the Board was allowed to treat that oral notice as sufficient and the employer was not prejudiced by the missing paperwork.

This article summarizes a published court decision in general terms and is not legal advice.

Curbelo was a foreperson for a construction company. On July 26, 2023, a crane positioned on top of the building where he was working caught fire and collapsed through the ceiling. He later said he was injured by falling rocks and debris.

In January 2024, he filed a claim for injuries to his head, neck, back, right shoulder, right wrist, right hand and right knee. The claim was ultimately established for the neck, back, right shoulder, right elbow, right wrist, right hand and right knee, and for postconcussion syndrome.

The notice fight under Section 18

Workers' Compensation Law Section 18 requires written notice of an injury to the employer within 30 days. The employer and its carrier, listed in the case as Neptune Mechanical Inc. and Monadnock Construction Inc., opposed the claim in part for untimely notice. Curbelo had not given timely written notice.

What he had done was talk. The Board credited his testimony that, immediately following the collapse, he informed his supervisor that everyone in his crew was safe and that he had been injured by falling rocks and debris. He did not miss any time from work at first. He sought medical attention a week later, and on August 22, 2023 he texted his supervisor that he had herniated discs in his back and neck and required emergency surgery.

A workers' comp judge found that Curbelo had provided timely notice and established the claim. The Workers' Compensation Board affirmed in a decision filed May 2, 2025. The employer and carrier appealed.

What the court decided on November 25

The Appellate Division, Third Department, decided Matter of Curbelo v. Neptune Mechanical Inc., No. CV-25-0891, on November 25, 2026, and affirmed.

On notice, the court deferred to the Board's credibility findings and restated the rule: the resolution of the sufficiency of a claimant's oral notice is a matter within the exclusive province of the Board. On this record, the Board's determination that the oral notice was sufficient was supported by substantial evidence.

The court also agreed the employer was not prejudiced by the lack of timely written notice. It was undisputed that the employer was aware of the crane accident. And Curbelo's medical records, beginning with his first medical visit after the accident, were available for review by the employer's own medical expert. The Board's finding that the missing written notice did not prevent a proper investigation was, in the court's words, plainly supported.

On causation, the court did the same kind of review. Curbelo's treating physician said the injuries were causally related to the crane accident. The orthopedic surgeon who examined him for the employer agreed that the neck, back, right shoulder, right wrist, right elbow and right hand injuries were causally related, and disputed only the right knee. The court noted that the Board can weigh conflicting medical evidence, credit one expert over another, and accept or reject portions of an expert's opinion. The Board's decision to establish the knee as well stood.

Two notice cases, two weeks apart, opposite results

This ruling lands less than two weeks after the same court affirmed the opposite result in Matter of Bailey, where a school bus driver's claim was disallowed because she failed to give timely notice under Section 18. Read together, the two cases draw the real line. The notice defense does not turn on the calendar alone. It turns on what the employer knew, when it knew it, and whether the late or missing written notice actually hurt the investigation.

In Bailey, the defense won. In Curbelo, the worker spoke to his supervisor at the scene of a major accident the employer already knew about, and his medical records were available from the first visit onward. There was nothing left for the prejudice argument to stand on.

Lesson one: assume the conversation at the scene will decide the case

Supervisors are the notice system in the first hour, whether the company treats them that way or not. If a worker tells a supervisor he is hurt, that conversation can become the notice the Board credits years later. Train supervisors to write down every injury report the day it happens, word for word, and to send it up the same day. A short written record made at the scene protects everyone. It proves what was reported, and it starts the investigation while the facts are still fresh.

Lesson two: prejudice has to be shown, not just claimed

Arguing that written notice was late is not enough when the employer knew about the accident and had the medical records. If your carrier plans to raise a Section 18 defense, the file needs to show what the delay actually cost: witnesses who could not be found, a scene that could not be inspected, records that were not available. If the investigation was not actually impaired, expect the Board to excuse the notice problem and move on to the merits.

Lesson three: concessions by your own expert narrow the causation fight

The employer's surgeon agreed that six of the seven established sites were causally related and disputed only the knee. Once that concession is on the record, the Board is free to credit the treating physician on the one disputed site and establish all of them. Before a hearing, know exactly which sites your expert supports and which he disputes, and make sure the defense strategy matches that map instead of arguing causation in general terms the record does not support.

What to do now

  • Make the supervisor's report automatic. Any statement by a worker that he or she is hurt gets written down and reported the same day, even if the worker finishes the shift and says he is fine.
  • Preserve the first-hour record. Incident reports, scene photos, texts and messages with supervisors, and the first medical records are the evidence a notice fight will be decided on.
  • Test a notice defense before you lean on it. Ask what the company knew at the time and what the delay actually prevented. If the honest answer is nothing, price the claim accordingly.
  • Map expert concessions early. Build the causation strategy around the sites your own expert actually disputes, not around a blanket denial.

The first hour is part of the claim file

Curbelo did not win because the 30 day written notice rule does not matter. Bailey, decided days earlier, shows that it does. Curbelo won because the facts around the rule were documented in his favor: a supervisor told at the scene, an accident the employer knew about, and medical records the employer's expert could review. Employers cannot control which of those facts a case will have. They can control whether their own supervisors create a clear record in the first hour, every time.
