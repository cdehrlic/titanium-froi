The Machine That Failed Twice

A dietary aide opened a commercial dishwasher and took a blast of scalding water. The safety switch that should have stopped the cycle had already failed once, on the same machine, less than two months before.

This story is a fictionalized composite based on real claims. Names and identifying details have been changed.

The end of a meal service in an institutional kitchen runs on muscle memory. Racks come out of the commercial dishwasher steaming, the next racks go in, and the door opens and closes a hundred times a shift without anyone thinking about it. The machine is built for that rhythm. A safety interlock in the door cuts the wash cycle when the door opens, so the person pulling the rack is never standing in front of live spray.

On this shift, a dietary aide opened the door and the cycle did not cut. Scalding water and steam hit her arm and face. She was hospitalized for more than a day with serious burns, the kind of injury that changes a claim file from routine to severe in a single phone call.

Then the file took the turn that makes this story worth telling. The interlock had not failed out of nowhere. It had failed before.

The failure was already on paper

When the machine was examined, the manufacturer and the servicing company confirmed the interlock was faulty. That alone would have made it a product problem. What made it an employer problem was the service history: the same machine, the same safety device, had failed less than two months earlier.

Read that again. A safety device whose only job is to stop the machine when a person opens it had already shown it could not be trusted. The machine went back into daily service anyway. Nobody tagged it out. Nobody put it on a watch list. Nobody treated a failed safety switch as what it is, which is a warning with a date on it.

Equipment rarely injures someone the first time it misbehaves. It warns first. A guard that sticks, a sensor that trips and resets, an interlock that works nine times out of ten. The injury happens on the day the warning and the workload line up, and by then the paperwork proving someone knew is already sitting in a service file.

A serious burn is never just a burn claim

A hospitalization of more than 24 hours pulls in machinery beyond the claim itself. It invites regulatory attention, because an injury this serious raises the obvious question of how the equipment was maintained. It also opens a second door that many employers never walk through: if a defective product caused the injury, part of the cost belongs to the manufacturer or the servicer, not to the employer's loss history.

That recovery has a name, subrogation, and it has one hard requirement. The evidence has to survive. A machine that gets repaired, adjusted, or hauled away by the vendor in the week after the injury takes the proof with it. The employers who recover in cases like this are the ones who photograph the equipment, keep the service records, and tell the vendor in writing that nothing gets touched until the investigation is done.

In this file the fault was confirmed and documented, which is more than many employers get. But notice what that documentation also does. It fixes the timeline. It shows the defect was real, it was known, and it predated the injury by weeks.

The rule a repeat failure should trigger

None of the fixes here are exotic. They are habits, and they are cheap compared to one hospitalization:

  • A safety device gets one free failure. The first time an interlock, guard switch, or sensor fails, it gets repaired and the repair gets verified under load, not just noted on a work order.
  • The second failure takes the machine out of service. Same device, same machine, inside a short window means the equipment waits for a real diagnosis before anyone works on it again. Production pressure does not get a vote.
  • Service reports get read by someone whose job includes safety. A vendor invoice that says a safety switch was replaced should land on a desk that understands what that sentence means for the next shift.
  • Repeat faults get logged in one place. If the first failure lives in a vendor portal and the second lives in a kitchen logbook, nobody ever sees the pattern. One list, reviewed monthly, is enough.

Employers tend to think of prevention as training, posters, and toolbox talks. Those matter. But a large share of serious injuries come from a shorter story: a machine said something was wrong, the message reached no one with the authority to act, and the machine kept running until it said it louder.

This dishwasher told the truth twice. The first time cost a service call. The second time cost a woman a hospital stay, and it will cost her employer years of premium. The difference between those two outcomes was never the machine. It was whether anyone treated the first failure as the end of the story instead of the beginning of one.
