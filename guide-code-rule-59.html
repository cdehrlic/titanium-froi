Got a Code Rule 59 Notice? What New York Employers Have to Do Next

A letter from NYCIRB saying you are subject to Code Rule 59 is not a suggestion. Your experience mod is over 1.20, your payroll is over $800,000, and state law now requires you to hire a certified safety consultant on a fixed timetable. Here is exactly what has to happen, and when.

This is a plain-English guide to New York Industrial Code Rule 59, the compulsory Workplace Safety and Loss Prevention program. It describes the process in general terms and is not legal advice. Deadlines and procedures come from the NY Department of Labor and NYCIRB; confirm the current requirements for your situation.

Most employers first hear about Code Rule 59 the same way: a notice arrives from the New York Compensation Insurance Rating Board, and suddenly there is a state program with your company's name on it. The notice means two things triggered at once. Your experience modification is above 1.20, which puts you in roughly the worst-performing slice of employers in the state, and your gross payroll is over $800,000. At that point the state does not ask you to improve your safety program. It orders a certified outsider to come evaluate it.

There is no point arguing with the notice. The thresholds are mechanical, and the program is compulsory. What you can control is how well you use it, because the same review the state is forcing on you is, done right, the safety-program overhaul most employers in your position needed anyway.

The deadlines start the day the notice arrives

Code Rule 59 runs on a strict clock, and missing a date carries its own penalty. The sequence works like this:

  • Within 30 days of the notice, you must select a safety consultant certified by the NY Department of Labor and schedule the consultation.
  • Within 10 days of selecting the consultant, you must notify the Department of Labor of the consultant's name, address, certification number, and the scheduled dates.
  • Within 75 days of the notice, the consultation and the on-site workplace evaluation must be finished and the report in your hands.
  • Within 30 days of receiving the report, you send a copy to your workers' comp carrier and to the Department of Labor, along with a letter describing how you will carry out the recommendations and on what timeline.
  • Within 6 months of receiving the report, the recommended corrective actions must be completed.
  • Within 60 days after that remediation period, your carrier inspects the workplace to verify compliance, and reports the result to you and the Department of Labor within 45 days of the reinspection.

Put the notice date on a calendar the day it arrives and work backward from every one of these. Employers get in trouble not because the requirements are unclear, but because 30 days passes faster than anyone expects while they are still deciding whom to hire.

What the consultant actually does

A Code Rule 59 consultation is not a walkthrough with a clipboard. The certified consultant must do two things: survey the entire workplace for hazards, and assess your existing workplace safety and loss prevention program against what the regulation requires. For employers with more than one worksite, the consultant reviews loss and claim information location by location, the same way they would for a single site.

The deliverable is a written report that identifies deficiencies in your safety program and recommends specific corrections. That report goes to the state and your carrier, so it becomes part of your permanent record with both. This is why the choice of consultant matters more than most employers realize. A thorough, credible consultant produces a report you can actually execute. A sloppy one produces findings you will be answering questions about for years.

One practical note: if you are a NYSIF policyholder, NYSIF will provide a certified consultant to perform the consultation at no additional cost. If you are insured in the private market, you hire and pay a private certified consultant yourself. The Department of Labor keeps a public list of certified consultants, and your carrier may have certified staff who perform these consultations for clients.

What ignoring it costs

The penalty for non-compliance is a premium surcharge, and it compounds. Fail to initiate the safety program or implement the consultant's recommendations and the carrier applies a 5% surcharge to your modified premium. Each successive year of non-compliance adds another 5%, so 5% becomes 10%, then 15%, and it keeps climbing. The surcharge is not experience-rated, which means it sits on top of the already-high premium your 1.20-plus mod is producing.

Do the rough math on your own premium and the surcharge alone usually exceeds the cost of hiring a good consultant and doing the work. Non-compliance is the most expensive option on the menu, every time.

How to pick the right consultant

The Department of Labor's certified-consultant list is long, and not every name on it is equal. Three questions separate the consultants worth hiring from the rest:

  • Do they do this specific work regularly? Code Rule 59 consultations have their own format, report structure, and state expectations. A general safety trainer who does one every few years is not the same as someone who lives in this program.
  • Will they tell you what the report will say before it is final? A good consultant holds a closing conference, walks you through the findings, and gives you a chance to address what you can before the report is filed. You want no surprises in a document that goes to the state.
  • Do they understand the claims side, not just the safety side? Your mod is over 1.20 because of losses, not just hazards. A consultant who can read a loss run and connect the safety findings to what is actually driving your claims gives you a far more useful report.

Use the notice as leverage, not just compliance

Here is the part most employers miss. The state is forcing a safety-program review, which is pillar one of any serious comp cost program: safety before the accident. But the reason you got the notice, a mod over 1.20, usually involves the other three pillars too. How fast injuries get reported and investigated, whether your carrier's reserves on open claims reflect reality, and whether your mod worksheet and class codes are even correct.

Smart employers treat the Code Rule 59 notice as the trigger for a full review of all four, not just the safety piece the state ordered. You are already paying for a consultant to be on site and already opening your program to scrutiny. That is the cheapest moment you will ever get to find out what else is leaking.
