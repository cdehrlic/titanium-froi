A Bill Sitting on the Way to the Governor Would Remove a Key Defense in NY Comp Claims

Five months after the New York State Senate passed it 60 to 0, Assembly Bill A8482A still has not been delivered to Governor Kathy Hochul, according to a November 4, 2026 advisory reporting the official legislative record. If it is signed, insurance carriers and employers will no longer be able to withhold, reduce, or condition an injured worker's benefits on proof that the worker is still looking for work.

This article summarizes pending legislation in general terms and is not legal advice. The bill described here has not been signed and is not law.

Labor market attachment is one of the few tools left in a long running permanent partial disability claim. A worker who cannot go back to his old job may still be able to do lighter work. Under current practice, a carrier can argue that benefits should be withheld or reduced if the worker is not genuinely looking for work within his restrictions, on the theory that he has voluntarily withdrawn from the labor market.

A8482A would take that argument away by statute. The bill, sponsored in the Assembly by Member of Assembly Cruz, with a Senate companion, S6376A, sponsored by Senator Scarcella-Spanton, amends Section 15 of the Workers' Compensation Law. The Senate version adds a new paragraph stating plainly that benefits or compensation otherwise due shall not be withheld, diminished, or conditioned upon an injured worker's demonstration of attachment to the labor market. If signed, the act takes effect immediately.

The defense was already narrowed by the courts this year

This bill does not arrive in a vacuum. In Blake v. Niagara Wheatfield Central School District, decided February 19, 2026, the Appellate Division, Third Department, held that once a claimant is classified as permanently partially disabled with a loss of wage earning capacity, ongoing labor market attachment is not required unless the carrier proves the claimant voluntarily withdrew from the workforce. A missing job search, by itself, was not enough.

The sponsor's memo makes a similar point about the older law. It notes that the attachment defense grew out of court decisions rather than statute, and that the 2017 amendments meant to limit it did not succeed. A8482A is the Legislature's attempt to settle the question directly, by removing attachment as a condition of benefits altogether rather than leaving it to case by case proof of voluntary withdrawal.

Why the December window matters

The timing is part of the story. Last year a comparable workers' comp bill, A2748, which would have expanded permanent total disability, reached the governor on December 8 and was vetoed four days later, on December 12, 2025. Reporting on that veto said the governor cited an unfunded liability of at least $800 million. A bill defining temporary total disability was also vetoed, on December 30, 2022.

A8482A heads into the same end of year window with stronger legislative support. It cleared the Senate without a dissenting vote, 60 to 0, while A2748 passed that chamber 42 to 16. Unanimous passage does not guarantee a signature, and the cost concerns raised in last year's veto have not gone away. It does mean employers should not treat this as a bill that stalled and died. It passed, it is waiting on delivery, and a decision could come with little warning because the law would take effect on signing.

What this would mean on an open claim file

Permanent partial disability awards in New York can run for years, with statutory caps ranging from 225 to 525 weeks depending on the loss of wage earning capacity. Attachment disputes are often the point where those long claims get tested: is the worker looking for suitable work, turning down suitable work, or simply waiting out the award?

If attachment can no longer be raised at all, that test disappears. Claims that might have been suspended or reduced for a failure to search would continue. Reserves on open permanent partial disability claims would need to reflect the longer expected life of the award, and those reserves feed the loss runs that drive your experience mod. The bill does not change the caps, the classification process, or the Board's power to reconsider the degree of impairment. It removes one specific lever, and it is a lever carriers use on some of the most expensive claims in the system.

What to do now, while the bill is still pending

  • Know your exposure. Ask your carrier or third party administrator how many of your open claims are permanent partial disability claims, and whether any benefits are currently suspended, reduced, or being litigated on attachment or voluntary withdrawal grounds. Those are the claims most affected if this is signed.
  • Keep return to work at the center. A documented offer of suitable light duty, made in writing and matched to the worker's medical restrictions, matters under current law and will still matter if the law changes. It is also the part of the process the employer controls directly.
  • Document, do not assume. Where a worker declines suitable work or leaves the workforce for reasons unrelated to the injury, keep the records. Under the Blake standard that proof is what a carrier needs today, and a clear file is valuable under either version of the law.
  • Watch the delivery, not just the headlines. The bill has not been delivered to the governor yet. Once it is delivered, the decision window is short, as last December showed. Ask whoever monitors your claims to flag delivery and the governor's action as soon as either happens.

A quiet bill with a long tail

There has been no signing ceremony and no press release on this bill. It passed one chamber unanimously, sits undelivered months later, and would change how the longest claims in the New York system are paid if it becomes law. That is exactly the kind of change employers tend to learn about from a reserve increase, months after the fact.
